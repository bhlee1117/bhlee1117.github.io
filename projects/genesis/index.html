Genesis

A shared cloning designer for the lab’s plasmid inventory

Indexes the plasmid files already sitting on the lab share, turns them into a searchable inventory, and plans cloning routes from it.

Why I built it

Our plasmid files live in thousands of folders, saved by different people over many years, in whatever format their software wrote. Finding whether a construct already exists is harder than it should be, and planning a clone means reading maps by eye.

Genesis reads that share as it is, rather than asking anyone to reorganize it.

What it does

It parses GenBank, ApE, SnapGene and FASTA files, normalizes mixed line endings, and collapses duplicates: a checksum that ignores rotation and strand means the same plasmid saved three times by three people becomes one entry with three sources. Sanger reads are kept but marked, so no design is ever planned off a raw trace.

Features are found two ways that fail differently — label rules over free text, and motif search over the sequence, with peptide motifs matched on the translation so codon choice does not hide them.

A construct is specified as an ordered list of modules. Genesis resolves each against the inventory, assembles the intended sequence, validates it, and returns ranked routes with a primer table and a step-by-step protocol.

Where the judgment is

The most useful route is usually a two-parent swap: find the closest existing plasmid, work out the one region that has to change, and find a donor for the new piece. If the closest parent already contains everything asked for, it says so and suggests checking whether the construct exists before cloning anything.

It declines to propose things that fail at the bench — a seven-fragment ligation, a 14 kb backbone PCR, a fragment too short to be worth amplifying — and says why instead of quietly offering them.

Primers are computed from the template rather than reused from annotations, and melting temperature is reported for the annealing region alone, since quoting the whole oligo including its assembly tail is a common reason a first PCR fails.

Constraints

Python standard library only, no third-party packages, so it runs on a lab machine with a stock install and no network. Everyone in the lab gets the same inventory and the same answers.
