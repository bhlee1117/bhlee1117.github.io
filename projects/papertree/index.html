PaperTree

A literature atlas built around claims, not papers

A reference manager answers “what have I read?”. This answers “what does the field claim, what holds it up, and where is the evidence thin?”

Open PaperTreeGitHub

Why I built it

Reading around dendritic computation, I kept rediscovering that a claim I had taken as settled rested on one preparation, or on one lab. That is visible in principle from a reference library and invisible in practice.

So the unit here is the claim, not the paper. Papers attach to claims as evidence that supports, qualifies, contradicts or assumes them.

How it works

A Zotero export goes in. The pipeline pulls metadata, labels each paper by preparation and measurement, and proposes which claims each paper bears on; the model chooses only from claims I have written, and cannot invent one.

The claims themselves are mine to write, in a hand-edited file. That is the part that is actually thinking, and the rest exists to keep it cheap to maintain: work is tracked per paper-and-claim pair, so adding a paper or rewording a claim re-runs only what changed.

The output is a single self-contained page with three views: the claim tree, an author–paper network where each paper hangs from its nearest predecessor, and a faceted browser over the whole library.

The part I care about

Each claim is checked for thin evidence: whether its positive case rests on a single direct source, or whether every supporting paper shares one preparation or one modality. That second test is the reason the factual labels exist at all.

Caveats are computed rather than annotated. A claim supported entirely by calcium imaging automatically inherits the caveat that calcium reports suprathreshold events rather than membrane potential, so adding a caveat later is one rule rather than an edit to every link.
